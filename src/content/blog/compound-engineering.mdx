Media tracking is fragmented. If you watch movies, series, *and* anime,
your history is probably split across several services:
[Letterboxd](https://letterboxd.com) for films,
[Serializd](https://www.serializd.com), [Trakt](https://trakt.tv), or
[Simkl](https://simkl.com) for TV, [AniList](https://anilist.co) for
anime. Each has its own
community and its own strengths, and none of them covers everything. So you
either commit to one service and lose the others, or you keep several and
log everything more than once. I was doing the second one, and it's exactly
as tedious as it sounds.

[Shinobu](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu) is my answer: a harness
over the trackers rather than yet another tracker. It's a single Expo
codebase that runs on the web, iOS, and Android — I use it daily on my
Android phone and in the browser, at
[shinobu.glpecile.xyz](https://shinobu.glpecile.xyz/) — and hopefully
coming soon to TestFlight.

Day to day it looks like any tracker: an **Up Next** feed with the next
unwatched episode of each show you follow (an episode only appears once it
has actually aired in your timezone), a **calendar** of upcoming releases,
and a **diary** of everything you've logged. The difference is where the
data lives. Shinobu has no account system, no backend, and no database —
every one of those screens is aggregated live from the trackers you've
connected, and every log is written back out to them.

Connecting is per-provider and opt-in. You link any subset of Simkl,
Trakt, AniList, Letterboxd, and Serializd through each provider's own
login, and the provider's token *is* the session — there's nothing else to
sign into:

When you log something, Shinobu writes it to every connected provider that
handles that kind of media, and the routing isn't one-to-one, because the
providers aren't symmetric.
Letterboxd only does film, Serializd only does TV, AniList lives entirely
in anime and manga. Try it:

<FanOutDemo client:only="react" />

Two details of this design matter for the rest of the story. First, all of
those routing rules live in
[one small, tested module](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/blob/main/src/lib/providers/routing.ts)
— the rest of the app just asks it where a log should go, so no screen
anywhere has its own opinion about which tracker handles what. Second,
failure stays visible: if
[Letterboxd is down](https://variety.com/2026/film/news/letterboxd-down-users-locked-out-accounts-1236830887/)
while the other four writes succeed, Shinobu tells you Letterboxd failed and hands you a link to log
the film there yourself, instead of pretending everything worked. The whole
design assumes any provider can be missing at any moment.

That assumption was made for resilience — networks fail, tokens expire. It
ended up mattering for a very different reason.

## Compound engineering

The other unusual thing about Shinobu is how it's built: most of its code
is written by AI agents. Early on, I ran into the phrase "compound
engineering" in a [Ben Awad video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jxq0CQk2rb0)
and followed it to [Every's guide](https://every.to/guides/compound-engineering)
by Kieran Klaassen. The whole idea fits in one sentence:

> Each unit of engineering work should make subsequent units easier — not
> harder.

The version of this I practice is mostly about writing things down. Models
remember a lot these days, but that memory belongs to a session, a tool, or
a vendor. A project outlives all three: work happens across hundreds of
sessions, in different tools, on whatever model is best this month. The
memory that compounds is the memory that lives in the repo — versioned,
greppable, readable by every future contributor regardless of what's
running it.

In Shinobu that memory has three parts:

- [`docs/plans/`](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/tree/main/docs/plans)
  — numbered blueprints, written *before* building.
- [`docs/solutions/`](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/tree/main/docs/solutions)
  — one file per solved bug or non-obvious finding.
- [`AGENTS.md`](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/blob/main/AGENTS.md) —
  the conventions file every session loads, which cites those solution
  files as the evidence behind its rules.

Two habits hold it together. Fixes get written up the same day they land,
because by tomorrow the details are gone. And every new plan starts with a
search through the solutions folder — even a search that finds nothing gets
noted, so nobody has to wonder later whether anyone looked. One naming
convention does a surprising amount of work: the title of a doc is a claim,
not a topic. Not
`season-mapping-notes.md`, but "ani.zip's seasons are TVDB's, and the
trackers' usually aren't." When titles state findings, a plain text search
returns lessons instead of homework.

## When a provider plays dirty

Even with that symmetric design, Shinobu had a de facto favorite. It was
built Trakt-first —
[plans 0006 through 0010](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/tree/main/docs/plans)
are all Trakt: OAuth, search, seasons, watch progress. Trakt was the spine that the Up Next feed, the
calendar, and TV tracking leaned on.

The first sign of trouble came in June 2026, when Trakt shipped breaking
API changes: images removed from responses, pagination made mandatory,
endpoints drifting from their own published docs. Annoying, but absorbable
— the kind of thing you document and move past.

Then, effective the week of July 22nd, 2026, the real change: free Trakt
accounts were capped at **one connected community app**. Connecting a
second app now requires VIP at $60/year — after VIP prices had already
risen 100–300% the year before. Overnight, every third-party app built on
Trakt's public API became something its users might have to pay Trakt to
keep using. r/trakt filled with people
[discovering the hard way that only one of their apps still worked](https://www.reddit.com/r/trakt/comments/1veosz1/1_app/),
and with
[developers whose API tokens were simply deleted](https://www.reddit.com/r/trakt/comments/1vb2gyc/api_deleted_vip_required_to_be_a_developer_now/)
until they, too, went VIP.

I want to be fair about this. Charging for an API is legitimate.
Infrastructure costs money, and $60 a year is not an outrageous number by
itself. If the change had been announced well ahead of time, priced at
developers instead of end users, or grandfathered existing connections, I
suspect most people would have grumbled and paid. Goodwill is a real
currency, and plenty of services keep loyal, paying users through honest
communication alone.

But that's not what happened. The cap landed on users of free apps,
retroactively, on top of a price hike and a summer of unannounced breaking
changes. And a move like that rests on a single assumption: that you're
hard to replace.

Trakt wasn't. On July 31st, 2026 came the decision: full detachment. Simkl
— free API, real OAuth, write parity — becomes the tracking spine. Trakt
stays supported, but demoted to bring-your-own-everything: the app ships no
Trakt credentials at all, and the provider activates only if a user
registers their own Trakt API app and pastes in their own keys. No bundled
key for the cap to hit. No dependency left to squeeze.

Making the decision took one conversation. What the decision *implies* is
replacing the primary integration of a five-provider app — reads, writes,
watchlist, calendar, OAuth, rate-limit handling, all of it. That's the kind
of migration that sits on a roadmap for a quarter.

It shipped the next day.

## Why it took a weekend

[The plan for the swap](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/blob/main/docs/plans/0034-simkl-provider-and-trakt-detachment.md)
— 322 lines, ten implementation units, written the same day as the decision
— did not start from a blank page. It opens with a search through the
solutions folder and cites what it found by name. Its retry design follows
"the AniList lesson" — a
[retry storm](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/blob/main/docs/solutions/anilist-rate-limit-retry-storm.md)
from two and a half weeks earlier, written up the day it was fixed. Its
live-account checks follow "the Serializd lesson" — a
[near-miss with destructive writes](https://github.com/glpecile/shinobu/blob/main/docs/solutions/serializd-watchlist-clears-watched.md)
documented the day before the decision. Neither had anything to do with
Trakt or Simkl when it was written; both were sitting in the folder, one
search away, exactly when the migration needed them. The plan also lists
stop conditions, agreed on in advance, where whoever is executing — human
or agent — has to stop and ask rather than guess: "if the live-account
probe shows Simkl's status model destroys watched state, stop."

Cap on July 22nd, 2026. Decision on July 31st. Plan written the same day.
Shipped August 1st, 2026. The ledger is honest about what
shipping fast looks like: four bug reports the next day, each becoming a
doc before its fix landed. The system doesn't promise flawless — it
promises that nothing gets learned twice.

That's the compounding the name points to. The expensive part of a rewrite
was never the typing. It was re-learning everything the codebase already
knew, and that cost had already been paid, one markdown file at a time, by
five weeks of ordinary work that left receipts.

## The cautionary tale

There are two lessons here, one for each side of an API.

If you run a platform that people build on: goodwill is load-bearing. Users
will pay a fair fee when they can see the reasoning and hear about it
before it hits them. What they won't do is stay put when the terms change
underneath them — and the cost of leaving keeps falling. Switching away
from a deeply integrated dependency used to take a quarter of engineering
time, and that inertia quietly protected a lot of bad platform behavior.
When a codebase remembers everything it has ever learned, that protection
thins out fast.

If you build on platforms: keep receipts. Write down every rate limit,
every quirk, every lesson, at the moment you learn it. Not because any
single note is precious, but because the pile is what makes you portable —
the difference between "we can't leave, the migration would kill us" and
"it shipped the next day."

The codebase remembers, so nobody — human or agent — has to learn anything
twice.
